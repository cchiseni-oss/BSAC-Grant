## Summary

From the mid-1890s until 1924, the territory that became Zambia was administered by a chartered, profit-seeking firm, the British South Africa Company (BSAC), under the supervision of British imperial officials. Company-states played a large part in the history of capitalism and empire (Phillips and Sharman 2020), and concession-company rule elsewhere in Central Africa has had lasting effects on development (Lowes and Montero 2021). Yet there is no consistent account of how the BSAC financed its administration north of the Zambezi, what it spent money on, or how its taxes affected African labour and migration. Quantitative research on colonial public finance in British Africa has grown quickly (Frankema 2010; Gardner 2012), but a standard source for it, the annual Blue Book, is available for Northern Rhodesia in the main digitised collection only from 1924, when the Crown took over. This project uses a newly digitised archive of the BSAC's administrative records (954 catalogue entries, more than 60,000 images) to (1) reconstruct the territory's revenue and expenditure under company rule, (2) estimate how direct taxation affected African labour supply and migration at district level, and (3) test whether the transfer from company to Crown rule changed fiscal policy. The project will be based at the University of Gothenburg, with archive access through the Southern African Institute for Policy and Research in Lusaka, and will publish an open, documented dataset and three journal articles.

## Popular science summary

For almost thirty years, a company whose shares were traded in London ran the government of what is now Zambia. The British South Africa Company collected taxes, paid officials, policed the population and built roads, while also trying to earn a return for its shareholders. How does a business behave when it is also the state? Did it tax Africans more heavily than neighbouring colonies run by the British government, and did it spend less on schools and health? Did people leave a district when its taxes rose? These questions have been hard to answer because the company's own administrative records have not been available in a form that allows systematic study. This project uses tens of thousands of newly digitised pages from the company's administration to build consistent figures on its taxes and spending, and makes them freely available to researchers in Zambia and elsewhere.

## 1. Purpose and aims

The purpose of the project is to explain how a profit-seeking chartered company financed and governed a territory, and what its fiscal choices meant for the African population. It does this with the BSAC's own administrative records for North-Eastern Rhodesia, North-Western Rhodesia and, after the two were merged in 1911, Northern Rhodesia.

The project has four aims:

1. Reconstruct annual public revenue (by source) and expenditure (by function) for the BSAC administrations from about 1900 to 1924, and link these series to the Crown-era Blue Books from 1924.
2. Build a district-level panel of direct taxation (tax rates, assessed taxpayers or huts, collections, arrears, exemptions, and tax shared with the Barotse authorities) and of population.
3. Use these data to answer the three research questions in Section 3.
4. Publish the data with full documentation and archive references, so that researchers in Zambia and elsewhere can use and check them.

## 2. Background and state of research

### 2.1 Colonial public finance in Africa

Over the past fifteen years, economic historians have rebuilt the public finances of British Africa from colonial statistical returns. Frankema (2010) compared tax levels across thirty-four British dominions, colonies and protectorates between 1870 and 1940 to ask how "extractive" colonial taxes were. Frankema (2011) asked whether British African governments maximised revenue or minimised effort. Gardner (2012) showed how colonial governments' efforts to balance their budgets shaped their relations with local political stakeholders and with the imperial government. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2014) found that, under comparable local conditions, British and French administrations behaved in very similar ways, which suggests that fiscal institutions responded to local constraints more than to metropolitan blueprints. Van Waijenburg (2018) showed that labour obligations were a large and largely unrecorded source of early colonial state income, and Bolt and Gardner (2020) showed that African elites shaped local tax institutions under indirect rule. Hillbom and Green (2019) provide a broader synthesis of African economic history.

The annual Blue Books, which colonial administrations compiled for the Colonial Office and which contain accounts of revenue and expenditure, are a standard source for this kind of work. In the most widely used digitised collection, however, Northern Rhodesia's series begins only in 1924, while Southern Rhodesia's begins in 1906 and Nyasaland's in 1904 (Stanford Libraries n.d.). Whether comparable annual returns were compiled for the northern territories before 1924, and how complete they are, is itself something this project will establish. Either way, the record of almost three decades of public finance is held mainly in the company's own files.

The territory's economy after company rule is better documented. The 1938 commission on Northern Rhodesia's finances (Pim and Milligan 1938), early national accounts for Northern Rhodesia and Nyasaland (Deane 1953) and a study of export-led growth from 1920 to 1960 (Baldwin 1966) all concentrate on the Crown period. To our knowledge, the company period has no equivalent quantitative record.

### 2.2 Company-states and the BSAC

Chartered companies were profit-seeking firms that also held sovereign powers of war, peace and rule (Phillips and Sharman 2020). How such a company governed can matter long after it has gone: Lowes and Montero (2021) find that areas of the Congo Free State granted to rubber concession companies have worse education, wealth and health outcomes today. The BSAC is a central case. It was initially the largest company in Rönnbäck and Broberg's (2019) sample of London-listed ventures operating in Central and Southern Africa, and it administered Southern Rhodesia until 1923 and Northern Rhodesia until 1924.

General histories describe the company period in Northern Rhodesia (Gann 1958, 1964; Roberts 1976), and Slinn (1971) traced the political role of the company's commercial concessions in the territory from 1890 to 1964. Recent business history, including work by members of this project team [keep this phrase only if Klas Rönnbäck is an applicant], has returned to the company. Rönnbäck and Broberg (2022) show how the BSAC moved from defending its charter to renegotiating its terms and eventually embracing the end of company rule in 1923–24. Rönnbäck and Ngoma (2024) show how it persuaded the imperial government to accept contested claims to land and minerals in Northern Rhodesia and Nyasaland. Koke (2024) reconstructs Southern Rhodesia's fiscal system under the BSAC between 1890 and 1922 and finds that the company's approach was gradual, experimental and sometimes ad hoc, with shifting definitions of "commercial" and "administrative" revenue that caused repeated conflict with settlers.

To our knowledge, no comparable fiscal reconstruction exists for Northern Rhodesia. This gap matters for an old argument about company incentives. Arrighi (1966) argued that in Southern Rhodesia the company's wish to recover its heavy initial outlays led it to promote settler development that would raise the value of its railway, mining and land assets. North of the Zambezi, the company was unenthusiastic about copper development while it governed, and large-scale copper mining developed only from the late 1920s (Butler 2007). Under those conditions the same profit motive could have produced either a minimal administration, run as cheaply as possible until mineral values rose, or spending targeted at infrastructure that raised the value of company assets. The two possibilities predict different levels, composition and geography of public spending, which this project can test.

### 2.3 Taxation, labour and migration

Historians of Central and Southern Africa have long linked colonial land and labour policies, including taxation, to labour migration and the origins of rural poverty (Arrighi 1970; Palmer and Parsons 1977). Studies of particular regions and sectors of Northern Rhodesia show how colonial rule, disease and war reshaped rural economies and labour supply: on the Tonga plateau (Vickery 1986), in the Northern Province (Moore and Vaughan 1994), in Mweru-Luapula, where Musambachime (1981) traced the social and economic effects of sleeping sickness between 1906 and 1922, and on the north-eastern plateau during the First World War, when carrier and food levies severely disrupted rural economies (Yorke 2015). On the labour market, Perrings (1979) studied African mineworkers on the Copperbelt from 1911, and Juif and Frankema (2018) show that copper mine workers in the Belgian Congo and Northern Rhodesia lived at bare-bones subsistence in the 1910s and 1920s before rapid welfare gains from the mid-1920s, as mining companies combined coercive recruitment with investment in living standards. Frankema and van Waijenburg (2012) find that urban real wages in British Africa were above subsistence and rising, with wide variation between colonies.

To our knowledge, what is missing for the company period is systematic, district-level evidence on how taxes were set and collected and how people responded. That evidence is needed to move beyond the general claim that taxation pushed Africans into wage labour, towards estimates of how large the response was, where it occurred, and whether it took the form of migration, wage work or evasion.

### 2.4 Approach

Cross-country studies have linked colonial institutions to long-run development (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson 2001), while historians have warned against the "compression of history" in such work (Austin 2008) and called on historians to engage with the new economic history of Africa written by economists (Hopkins 2009). Fourie (2016) argues that new advances in transcribing and linking archival records are especially valuable for regions with few written records, such as sub-Saharan Africa, and Jerven (2013) shows why official African statistics must be read critically. The project follows this approach: it builds dated, place-specific data from primary sources and treats colonial figures as evidence to be tested rather than as facts.

## 3. Research questions and hypotheses

**RQ1. How did the BSAC finance its administration north of the Zambezi?** What were the level, composition and trend of revenue (direct taxes on Africans, customs, licences and fees, and "commercial" revenue from land and minerals) and of expenditure (general administration, police and defence, justice, public works, health and education)? Where did the company draw the line between its administrative and commercial accounts, and did that line move over time, as Koke (2024) found for Southern Rhodesia? We test two competing hypotheses about a profit-seeking company-state:

- *H1a (minimal administration):* the company kept spending as low as possible and financed it mainly from direct taxes on Africans, so that spending per head on services was lower than in neighbouring Crown territories such as Nyasaland at comparable dates.
- *H1b (asset value):* the company spent more where spending raised the value of its land, railway and mineral interests, so that spending was concentrated along the railway line and in mining districts.

**RQ2. How did direct taxation affect African labour supply and migration?** Using district-level variation in tax rates, assessment and collection, we estimate how Africans responded to tax changes: by moving to lower-taxed districts or across borders, by seeking wage work in Southern Rhodesia or Katanga, or by evading assessment. One episode is documented directly. An out-letter file in the High Commissioner series on the increase of native tax in the Kasempa, Ndola and Luangwa districts is described in the catalogue as covering an "exodus of native to under taxed areas" (A2/1/4, Tag 307). We also study the share of tax paid to the Barotse authorities (IN1/1/5; A3/24/8; B1/104/2) as a case of fiscal bargaining with African rulers (compare Bolt and Gardner 2020).

- *H2:* tax increases reduced the registered taxpaying population of the affected districts relative to comparable districts, and increased recorded labour migration.

**RQ3. Did the end of company rule change fiscal policy?** We link the company-era series to Northern Rhodesia's Blue Books from 1924 and test for breaks in the level and mix of revenue and spending when the Crown took over. We concentrate on the years immediately after 1924, before large-scale copper mining developed (Butler 2007), to separate the change of ruler from the copper boom. We also document the fiscal terms of the transfer: the company's claims for wartime expenditure (A3/7/5, Tag 813), the supplemental charter (A3/5/2), and the proposed sale and the agreement between the Crown and the company (HC/1/3/68, HC/1/3/69 and HC/1/3/76). This extends Rönnbäck and Broberg's (2022) study of the company's negotiating strategy by measuring what was at stake financially.

- *H3:* the transfer to Crown rule was followed by a measurable shift in the revenue mix and in spending priorities.

## 4. Sources, data and methods

### 4.1 The archive

The main source is a digitised archive of the BSAC's administrative records for North-Eastern Rhodesia (from 1896), North-Western Rhodesia and Northern Rhodesia (1911–24), with some later material. The project has access to the digitised archive through the Southern African Institute for Policy and Research (SAIPAR), an independent research centre in Lusaka, and has permission to publish from it. Its catalogue lists 954 entries (files and volumes), and the archive contains more than 60,000 images. It includes correspondence between the territorial administrators and the company's London Office, the High Commissioner for South Africa and H.M. Commissioner in Zomba; district and sub-district reports; census and hut-tax returns; customs reports; and court records.

*Preliminary work.* We have reviewed all 954 catalogue entries and mapped 494 of them to 64 economic history research questions, with archive references for each. From this review, 103 catalogue entries are prioritised for this project (Annex A). They include:

- accounts, estimates and comparative statements of revenue and expenditure (for example A2/4/1/1, A2/4/3/1 and A1/1/52);
- district and sub-district reports on revenue, expenditure, census, trade and agriculture for North-Western Rhodesia in 1909–10 (A5/2/5 to A5/2/10);
- census and hut-tax records for villages in Luapula district (A9/1, Vols I–III);
- reports of the Controller of Customs, including comparative statements of imported goods (A5/1/3);
- correspondence on native tax, tax increases and tax-sharing (the HC1/2 series; A2/1/3 and A2/1/4, Tags 306–307; IN1/1/5; B1/104/1–2);
- Katanga native labour reports and labour recruitment records (A4/1/3, Vols I–III; A2/1/7); and
- files on war expenditure, the supplemental charter and the transfer to Crown rule (A3/7/5, Tag 813; A3/5/2; the HC/1/3 series).

References in the text are the catalogue's Ref. Nos. Where a Ref. No. occurs more than once in the catalogue, the Tag No. is given as well.

### 4.2 Building the datasets

The project will build three datasets:

- **D1. Territory-level public finance, annual, about 1900–1924.** Revenue by source and expenditure by function, in current pounds sterling, linked to the Blue Book series from 1924. Categories will be harmonised with those used in comparative studies (Frankema 2010, 2011; Gardner 2012) through a documented crosswalk.
- **D2. District-year panel of direct taxation and population.** Tax rates, assessed taxpayers or huts, collections, arrears, exemptions and shares paid to African authorities, together with population from census returns and district reports.
- **D3. Labour and migration indicators.** Recruitment returns, labour bureau records, Katanga labour reports and wartime carrier levies (A2/3/3–4), coded by district and year where possible.

*Transcription protocol.* A three-month pilot (WP0) on the 1909–10 district reports and the Luapula registers will measure how much usable quantitative information the files contain and how long transcription takes. The scope of D2 and D3 will be fixed after the pilot. Tabular material will be entered twice by two trained research assistants, and disagreements will be resolved against the image. We will test handwritten text recognition on typed and clearly written series and use it only where its error rate, measured on a double-entered sample, is below [threshold]. Every value will carry its archive reference (Ref. No., Tag No., Loc. No.) and image number, so that any figure can be traced to its source.

*Source criticism.* Colonial statistics are products of administration. A fall in the number of registered huts can reflect migration, evasion, death or a change in enforcement (Jerven 2013). We will check tax data against district narrative reports, medical and sleeping-sickness reports (which record resettlement; see Musambachime 1981) and post-1924 data, and we will report uncertainty explicitly. The catalogue itself records gaps in some files (for example A1/1/2, Tag 101; A1/4/2/1; A1/4/3/2), and many entries have no year. We will date undated documents from their content and record how each date was established.

### 4.3 Analysis

- **RQ1.** We describe the level, composition and trend of revenue and expenditure, overall and per head, and compare them with Nyasaland and with published series for other British African territories (Frankema 2010; Gardner 2012; Frankema and van Waijenburg 2014). To separate H1a from H1b, we compare the geography and composition of spending between districts on and off the railway line and between mining and non-mining districts. Correspondence between the administrators and the London Office on budgets and cost-cutting (for example A3/31/4, on a salary reduction ordered by the Board of Directors) shows how the company took these decisions.
- **RQ2.** With the district-year panel, we use an event-study (difference-in-differences) design that compares districts where tax rates rose with comparable districts where they did not, before and after the change. Outcomes are registered taxpayers, tax collected and recorded labour migration. We check for differences in pre-change trends, run placebo tests with false dates, and control for sleeping-sickness resettlement and wartime levies. Correspondence about each episode helps distinguish migration, evasion and mortality as mechanisms.
- **RQ3.** We test for structural breaks at 1924 in the levels and shares of revenue and spending, concentrating on the years up to about 1928, and analyse the transfer files qualitatively.

## 6. Significance and contribution

- **Economic history.** The project adds a company-administered territory to the comparative evidence on colonial public finance and tests competing hypotheses about how a profit-seeking ruler sets taxes and spending.
- **Business history.** It provides evidence from inside a company that was also a government, building on recent work on the BSAC's diplomacy and its claims to land and minerals (Rönnbäck and Broberg 2022; Rönnbäck and Ngoma 2024).
- **Long-run development.** It provides dated, place-specific evidence for debates on whether and how colonial institutions persisted (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson 2001; Lowes and Montero 2021), while avoiding the "compression of history" that Austin (2008) criticises.
- **Zambian history and research capacity.** It makes a key archive usable for quantitative research, trains Zambian research assistants in archival data methods, and returns the data to Zambian researchers through an open release and a workshop in Lusaka [co-organised with SAIPAR: confirm].
