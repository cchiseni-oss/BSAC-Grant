## Summary

Between about 1890 and 1924, the British South Africa Company (BSAC), a chartered, profit-seeking firm, conquered and then governed the territory that became Zambia. Company-states played a large part in the history of empire (Phillips and Sharman 2020), and recent research shows that Africans shaped colonial institutions, including taxation (Bolt and Gardner 2020). Yet how Africans resisted the company's occupation and rule in this territory, and how that resistance shaped the company-state, has received little systematic study. The older literature held that the territory offered little "primary resistance" to colonial rule, apart from episodes such as the Ngoni war of 1897–98 (Barnes 1954; Rotberg 1966). That view rests on a narrow definition of resistance and on scattered sources. This project combines a newly digitised archive of the company's administrative records (954 catalogue entries, more than 60,000 images) with records in Britain and Malawi to (1) map resistance in all its forms, from armed conflict to flight, evasion, desertion, religious protest and petitions; (2) document how the company responded, through military action, collective punishment, village removals and disarmament, and what this cost; and (3) test whether resistance and bargaining shaped the company's tax institutions. The project will be based at the University of Gothenburg and will publish an open, documented dataset of resistance and coercion events and three journal articles.

## Popular science summary

For about thirty years, a company whose shares were traded in London conquered and then ran what is now Zambia. Histories of the country often say that its peoples put up little resistance. But resistance takes many forms. People can take up arms, but they can also move away to avoid a new tax, refuse to work, desert the police, join a religious movement that rejects colonial authority, or petition for better terms. This project uses tens of thousands of newly digitised pages from the company's own administration, together with records in Britain and Malawi, to find out where and how people resisted, how the company responded, and whether resistance changed the way the company taxed and governed. The results will be shared as an open dataset for researchers in Zambia and elsewhere.

## 1. Purpose and aims

The purpose of the project is to explain how African resistance and bargaining shaped the establishment of company rule in the territories of North-Eastern Rhodesia, North-Western Rhodesia and, after 1911, Northern Rhodesia.

The project has four aims:

1. Build a dataset of resistance episodes and of company coercion, coded by place, date, form and outcome, from archives in Zambia, Britain and Malawi.
2. Explain how the company responded to resistance and what that response cost it.
3. Test whether resistance and bargaining changed the company's tax institutions: rates, methods of collection, exemptions and the use of African intermediaries.
4. Publish the data with full documentation and archive references.

## 2. Background and state of research

### 2.1 Resistance to company rule in Central Africa

The best-known case of resistance to the BSAC is the 1896–97 risings in Southern Rhodesia, which Ranger (1967) studied as a pioneering work on African resistance. Ranger (1968) went on to argue for connections between such "primary resistance" movements and later mass nationalism in East and Central Africa. North of the Zambezi, the conquest is usually described differently. The company fought the Ngoni of Mpezeni in 1897–98 (Barnes 1954) and campaigned against Arab and Swahili traders and their allies in Bemba country in the 1890s (Roberts 1973). Beyond these episodes, the older literature describes the establishment of rule as largely unopposed, with protest emerging later in religious and political forms (Rotberg 1966; Meebelo 1971). Meebelo (1971) traced African reactions to colonialism in northern Zambia from 1893 to 1939, and Yorke (2015) showed how carrier and food levies during the First World War caused resistance, desertion and the spread of the anti-colonial Watch Tower movement.

What is missing, to our knowledge, is a systematic account of resistance across the whole territory and the whole company period, built from the company's own administrative records. Without it, the claim that there was little resistance cannot be tested, and the many forms of everyday resistance, such as flight from taxation, evasion and desertion, remain largely invisible.

### 2.2 African agency and colonial institutions

Economic historians increasingly argue that African responses shaped colonial institutions. Bolt and Gardner (2020) show that the interaction of colonial officials and African elites created substantial variation in local tax institutions under indirect rule, using evidence from around 1950. Gardner (2012) shows how the need to balance colonial budgets shaped relations with local political stakeholders, and van Waijenburg (2018) shows that labour obligations were a large and often unrecorded source of early colonial state revenue. Frankema (2010) compares tax burdens across the British Empire. How resistance shaped colonial institutions at the moment they were created, rather than decades later, remains much less studied. Company-ruled territories are a particularly informative case, because the ruler was a firm that had to weigh the cost of coercion against shareholders' returns (Phillips and Sharman 2020). How such rule worked can have lasting effects: Lowes and Montero (2021) find that areas of the Congo Free State granted to rubber concession companies, where coercion was severe, have worse development outcomes today.

### 2.3 The BSAC north of the Zambezi

General histories describe the company period in Northern Rhodesia (Gann 1958; Roberts 1976). Recent business history has examined the company's diplomacy at the end of chartered rule (Rönnbäck and Broberg 2022) and its claims to land and minerals (Rönnbäck and Ngoma 2024). Koke (2024) has reconstructed the company's fiscal system in Southern Rhodesia. This project does not reconstruct the company's finances. It uses taxation as the measurable outcome of a different question: how the company-state was shaped by the people it sought to rule. Regional studies show how colonial rule, disease and war reshaped rural economies and the movement of people (Vickery 1986; Musambachime 1981), and Juif and Frankema (2018) show how mining companies in the Belgian Congo and Northern Rhodesia combined coercive recruitment with later investment in living standards.

### 2.4 Approach

Fourie (2016) argues that new advances in transcribing and linking archival records are especially valuable for regions with few written records, and Jerven (2013) shows why official African statistics must be read critically. Colonial records describe resistance in the language of officials, as "unrest", "outrages" or "disturbances". The project treats these records as evidence of both African action and official perception, and checks them across archives.

## 3. Research questions and hypotheses

**RQ1. Where, when and in what forms did Africans resist the company's occupation and rule?** We code episodes of armed conflict, raids, attacks on officials and property, flight and migration, tax evasion, desertion from the police, religious protest (notably the Watch Tower movement), and petitions. We test the claim that the territory saw little resistance (Rotberg 1966):

- *H1:* resistance was more widespread than the older literature suggests, but mostly took forms other than armed conflict, such as flight, evasion and petitioning.

**RQ2. How did the company respond, and what did resistance cost it?** We document military expeditions, police patrols, collective punishment, village removals, disarmament and gun registration, and changes in policing. Where the sources allow, we estimate the cost of these responses, including the company's wartime expenditure claims.

- *H2:* because it was a profit-seeking firm, the company preferred cheap forms of coercion (collective fines, removals, delegation to African intermediaries) to costly military campaigns, except when its authority was directly threatened.

**RQ3. Did resistance and bargaining shape the company's tax institutions?** Several Zambian files link resistance and taxation directly. One describes an "exodus of native to under taxed areas" after a tax increase in the Kasempa, Ndola and Luangwa districts (A2/1/4, Tag 307). Another records a taxpayer killed during disturbances in the Kafue district (A3/24/6 Vol. II). Others concern the collection of hut tax and tribute through Lozi indunas (IN1/1/5) and the share of tax paid to Lewanika (A3/24/8; B1/104/2), and tax exemptions (HC/1/3/73; HC/1/3/87). We test whether areas with more resistance, or stronger African authorities, received different tax rates, collection methods or exemptions, extending the argument of Bolt and Gardner (2020) to the founding period.

- *H3:* the company adjusted tax rates and collection to resistance and flight, and relied more on African intermediaries, with shared revenue, where African authorities were strong.

## 4. Sources, data and methods

### 4.1 The digitised BSAC archive (Zambia)

The core source is a digitised archive of the BSAC's administrative records for North-Eastern Rhodesia (from 1896), North-Western Rhodesia and Northern Rhodesia (1911–24). The project has access to it through the Southern African Institute for Policy and Research (SAIPAR), an independent research centre in Lusaka, and has permission to publish from it. Its catalogue lists 954 entries, and the archive contains more than 60,000 images.

*Preliminary work.* We have reviewed all 954 catalogue entries. Evidence of resistance to the initial occupation is thin in this collection, which is expected: in the 1890s North-Eastern Rhodesia was administered partly from Blantyre and Zomba in the British Central Africa Protectorate. The archive is much richer on the period when company rule was being established. Annex A lists 84 prioritised entries in six groups:

- early conflict in North-Eastern Rhodesia, including the collector's reports from Ikawa on the Arab slave trade (A1/1/1–2) and reports on the "settlement of Awemba country" (A8/2);
- disturbances in North-Western Rhodesia, including the unrest in Kafue district, the destruction of a mission store and a taxpayer killed (A3/24/6, Vols I–II), unrest at Lealui in 1911 (HC1/2/52, /53, /55) and the removal of "unrest natives" in Kafue (HC1/2/59, /60, /65);
- wartime and later protest, including files on an "alleged native rebellion" (HC/1/3/32, Vols I–II), the Watch Tower movement (HC/1/3/49–50) and police desertions (G3/1/2);
- company coercion, including collective punishment proclamations (A2/1/21; A2/1/35), fines on villages (HC/1/3/59), village removals (HC/1/3/14; A8/2/2, Vols I–III) and gun registration (A3/30; A3/24/5; B1/1/8);
- bargaining and petitions by African authorities, mainly in Barotseland (B1/2/2, /6, /11; HC/1/3/46); and
- taxation, as the outcome studied under RQ3.

These assessments rest on catalogue descriptions. Their content will be checked in the pilot (WP0).

### 4.2 Records in Britain and Malawi

The occupation itself is expected to be documented mainly outside Zambia. We will scope and use:

- **The National Archives, Kew.** Foreign Office correspondence relating to the British Central Africa Protectorate, which covered North-Eastern Rhodesia in the 1890s (series FO 2, to 1905), and Colonial Office correspondence on the company's relations with the British government, which includes copies of the company's administrative minutes and agenda up to 1924.
- **Derbyshire Record Office.** The papers of Philip Lyttelton Gell, a director (1899–1917, 1923–25), chairman (1917–20) and president (1920–23) of the BSAC, which include papers circulated to the company's directors (collection D3287/BSA). These show how the board in London learned of and responded to events in the territories.
- **National Archives of Malawi, Zomba.** Records of the British Central Africa Protectorate, which administered parts of North-Eastern Rhodesia in its early years.
- **Bodleian Library, Oxford.** A microfilm collection of letters from the BSAC's London office.

### 4.3 Building the datasets

- **D1. Resistance events.** Each episode is coded by date, district, people involved, form of resistance (armed conflict, raid, attack on officials or property, flight, evasion, desertion, religious protest, petition), the company's response, and outcome. Every entry carries its archive reference.
- **D2. Coercion events.** Military expeditions, patrols, collective punishments, fines, removals, disarmament measures and changes in policing, coded in the same way, with costs where recorded.
- **D3. Tax institutions by district and year.** Tax rates, methods of collection, use of African intermediaries, revenue sharing and exemptions, with tax collected and registered taxpayers where the figures survive.

Because the same event may be reported in several archives, each episode will be matched across sources, and conflicting accounts will be recorded rather than resolved silently. The pilot will establish how much numerical tax data survives. D3 can rest on descriptive evidence of tax rules and collection methods if long numerical series are not available.

### 4.4 Analysis

- **RQ1.** We describe the geography, timing and forms of resistance and compare them with the older literature's account. We use the cross-archive matching to assess how far each archive under- or over-reports resistance.
- **RQ2.** We study the company's choice of responses across episodes and how it changed over time, using the board papers and Colonial Office correspondence to show how decisions were made in London.
- **RQ3.** We compare tax institutions between districts with and without recorded resistance, and before and after major episodes, while accounting for other influences such as sleeping sickness resettlement, wartime levies and proximity to the railway. Where district-level tax figures survive, we use an event-study design around tax changes and resistance episodes. Case studies of the episodes named in Section 3 test the proposed mechanisms.

## 6. Significance and contribution

- **African and economic history.** The project tests a long-standing claim about the lack of resistance in the territory and makes everyday forms of resistance measurable.
- **Colonial institutions.** It extends research on how Africans shaped colonial institutions (Bolt and Gardner 2020) to the moment those institutions were created.
- **Business history.** It shows how a profit-seeking company-state weighed the costs of coercion, building on recent work on the BSAC (Rönnbäck and Broberg 2022; Rönnbäck and Ngoma 2024).
- **Research capacity.** It makes a key Zambian archive usable for systematic research, trains research assistants in Lusaka, and returns the data to Zambian researchers.

## 9. Ethics, data management and access

- The records date mainly from the 1890s to 1924, and the people named in them are almost certainly deceased. The EU General Data Protection Regulation does not apply to the personal data of deceased persons (GDPR, Recital 27). We will confirm with the University of Gothenburg's research ethics and data protection advisers whether any review is required.
- Colonial records of resistance describe violence, punishment and executions, often in derogatory language. We will publish event-level data without personal details beyond those already in published sources, and we will discuss the dataset's design with Zambian historians at the Lusaka workshop.
- The project accesses the digitised archive through SAIPAR and has permission to publish from it. Use of records in Kew, Derbyshire and Zomba will follow each archive's conditions.
